1. Why water efficiency needs a new lens

The crisis is about timing, quality, and allocation.

Conventional water efficiency often focuses on pipes, pumps, and canals. NbS adds a watershed view: reduce avoidable demand, hold rain where it falls, recharge groundwater, and protect water quality before treatment becomes expensive.

  • Global water withdrawals tripled over the last 50 years.
  • Population growth adds about 64 billion m3 of freshwater demand each year.
  • By 2030, 47% of the world population is projected to live in high water stress areas.

2. What counts as NbS?

Nature is not decoration. It is infrastructure.

Rey et al. define Nature Based Solutions as actions that protect, sustainably manage, and restore ecosystems while responding to societal challenges and conserving biodiversity.

  • Water quantity: recharge aquifers, retain stormwater, sustain stream flow.
  • Water quality: filter sediments, nutrients, metals, hydrocarbons, and pesticides.
  • Risk reduction: lower flood peaks, drought impacts, erosion, and heat stress.
  • Co benefits: habitat, cooling, recreation, landscape quality, and public health.
3. How NbS improves water efficiency

Efficiency means more useful water, not just less water.

The most important move is to change the pathway of rain: from fast runoff and polluted discharge to slow storage, infiltration, filtration, and reuse.

  • Vegetation and roots increase infiltration and soil water holding capacity.
  • Wetlands, swales, ponds, and floodplains delay peak flows.
  • Soil, plants, microbes, and sediments treat water before it reaches rivers.
  • Recharge areas keep groundwater available during dry periods.

6. The risk landscape by 2050

Climate change makes water risk systemic.

The TNC and IIASA report links water, food, and biodiversity risks under future climate scenarios. These numbers give the urgency behind scaling NbS.

Drought frequency

25% of global land area is projected to see drought frequency increase by over 70%.

Surface water stress

36% of land will experience surface water stress; 86% of that stressed area is severe.

Groundwater stress

Global groundwater stress is expected to increase 30% by 2050.

Nutrient pollution

Up to 40% of Earth's surface may exceed nitrogen and phosphorus ecological thresholds.

Biodiversity hotspots

64% of freshwater biodiversity hotspots are at risk of water stress, mostly outside protected areas.

Crop nutrients

Up to 25% of global crop nutrient production may be at risk, including zinc, iron, and vitamin A.

7. Agriculture as the central water user

Food security and water security are the same conversation.

Irrigation is productive, but it concentrates water risk. Efficient use requires soil water conservation on farms and landscape measures that keep rivers and aquifers functioning.

9. NbS for water quantity

Drought and depletion are managed from farm to basin.

Meanders and floodplains slow water and increase exchange with soils and aquifers.
Hybrid systems combine built control with natural storage and habitat.

On farm practices

Reduce irrigation demand and improve soil storage.

  • Low water use crops and crop diversification
  • Mulching, biochar, cover crops, and residue management
  • Reduced or no tillage
  • Drip, deficit, and alternate wet dry irrigation
  • Rotational grazing and grassland restoration

Landscape practices

Recharge water bodies and sustain baseflow.

  • Wetland protection and restoration
  • Riparian buffers and buffer strips
  • Reconnecting rivers to floodplains
  • Natural water retention and recharge
  • Managed aquifer recharge
10. NbS for water quality

The cheapest pollutant to treat is the one that never reaches the river.

Nutrient and sediment pollution rises when soil is exposed, fertilizer is mistimed, and runoff moves too quickly. NbS creates physical and biological filters across the system.

  • On farms: precision nutrient management, cover crops, intercropping, reduced tillage.
  • Across landscapes: filter strips, two stage ditches, riparian buffers, wetlands, floodplain restoration.
  • In water bodies: bivalve restoration and submerged aquatic vegetation.

Chesapeake Bay example

A reported suite of on farm practices reduced runoff of nitrogen, phosphorus, and sediment, while restored hectares retain additional pollutants before they enter the Bay.

11. NbS for flood risk

Floodplains are storage, habitat, and insurance.

Floodplain reconnection creates room for water during peak events, reduces downstream pressure, and supports biodiversity. It is especially powerful when designed with agriculture rather than against it.

24%

of global croplands are in flood prone areas

  • Floodplains regulate flow and recharge groundwater.
  • Riparian buffers reduce erosion and create habitat corridors.
  • Agroforestry and continuous ground cover increase roughness and soil stability.
  • Reservoir and lake operations can be aligned with floodplain restoration.
12. Urban water management

Cities can shift from drainage to sponge behavior.

In urban areas, NbS manages rainfall through retention, infiltration, evapotranspiration, treatment, and reuse. This can reduce the size and cost of collection networks and wastewater treatment needs.

13. Governance is part of the solution

NbS fails when treated as a one time construction project.

Living infrastructure changes over time. It needs planning, monitoring, consultation, maintenance, and adaptive management.

Planning

Coherent actions, optimized allocation, long term vision.

Monitoring

Ecological indicators, water quality data, public reporting.

Consultation

Workshops, local knowledge, shared governance, legitimacy.

Three dimensions that make NbS effective, sustainable, and socially accepted.
14. Implementation roadmap

From isolated projects to a water resilient landscape.

A practical NbS program should move through diagnosis, co design, financing, implementation, and adaptive management.

Diagnose the watershed

Map risks: drought, surface water stress, groundwater depletion, pollution, and flood exposure.

Choose the archetype

Match NbS to the dominant water challenge and production system.

Co design locally

Bring farmers, utilities, municipalities, communities, engineers, and ecologists together.

Blend finance

Use public incentives, grants, water funds, ecosystem service payments, and risk reduction funding.

Monitor and adapt

Track water quantity, water quality, biodiversity, maintenance needs, social acceptance, and costs.

15. Limits and design cautions

NbS is powerful, but not magic.

The sources repeatedly stress that NbS is context dependent and must often be combined with grey infrastructure, rules, maintenance, and monitoring.

Questions every project should answer

  • What events can it handle: normal storms, drought, extreme rainfall, or all of these?
  • What is the right scale: street, farm, city, aquifer, watershed, or basin?
  • Who maintains living systems as vegetation, soils, pests, and climate change over time?
  • How are tradeoffs handled between water use, biodiversity, agriculture, property, and equity?
  • Which grey infrastructure remains necessary for safety and reliability?

reduce demand + slow runoff + store water + filter pollution + recharge aquifers + govern fairly
16. Final message

Design with the water cycle, not against it.

  • NbS makes water efficiency spatial: the watershed becomes the unit of design.
  • The best projects deliver multiple benefits at once: supply security, risk reduction, quality improvement, biodiversity, and social value.
  • Scaling NbS depends on enabling conditions: policy, finance, evidence, monitoring, and inclusive collaboration.
